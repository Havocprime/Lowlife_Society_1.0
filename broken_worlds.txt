Prisoner’s Choice
Chapter One — Ashes and Hunger

The sky had the color of old bruises.

Kade squinted against the ash drifting like lazy snowflakes, his stomach twisting in on itself with every step. The city was dead, a skeleton of concrete and glass, and he was a maggot picking through the bones.

“Breakfast of kings,” he muttered, lifting the corner of a scorched vending machine with two good fingers and three stumps. A rat darted out. He tried not to envy it.

Inside, he found the half-melted wrapper of something that might once have been chocolate. His mouth watered despite himself. He tore it open, sniffed—plastic, ash, maybe rat piss. Still, he chewed. Hunger made philosophers of everyone: the question was never is it food? but can I swallow it before gagging?

He sat on the cracked sidewalk, back to a wall tagged with graffiti that had survived more bombs than its painters. His teeth ached, the gaps reminding him of every fight lost, every barter gone wrong. Still handsome, he liked to think—if you could ignore the missing parts.

Above, a drone buzzed faintly, like a mosquito circling the ruins. Most were dead these days, their factories long since blasted to rubble, but some still lingered, wandering the skies without masters. Production over precision—that was how the wars were fought. Thousands of cheap drones, each one a grenade with wings. The world was acne, bursting, bleeding, scarring over in all the wrong places.

Kade spat out a shard of wrapper. “Here’s to progress.”

The sound of boots scraped on gravel. He froze, every nerve taut.

Not drones. Not rats. People.

He ducked behind the vending machine carcass, heart thudding. Raiders? Scavs? Or worse—faction patrols. He’d rather take his chances with a half-dead drone than another human with a gun.

The footsteps grew louder. Voices, low but sharp, carried through the wreckage.

“…north sector’s clear.”
“…check again. Command wants survivors.”

Kade cursed under his breath. Survivors meant prisoners. Prisoners meant execution—or slavery, which was just slower execution with chores.

He tightened his grip on the rusted pipe strapped to his back. Not much of a weapon, but in the ruins, even steel made you dangerous enough to be feared. Sometimes.

The footsteps stopped. A shadow fell over him.

“You lost, scav?”

Kade looked up, pipe half-drawn—and froze.

The woman staring down at him wasn’t what he expected. Armor pieced together from drone plating, eyes bright even in the ash gloom. She had the kind of posture that spoke of discipline—military once, maybe. And a crossbow leveled straight at his chest.

Kade sighed. “Guess breakfast is over.”

The crossbow didn’t waver.

“Stand,” the woman said.

“Normally,” Kade replied, “I like to be bought dinner first.”

“Stand,” she repeated, sharper.

He rose slowly, pipe held out by two fingers. Three other figures flowed out of the rubble behind her—lean, alert, not amateurs. One wore a mask made from a drone’s camera dome, black and glossy like an insect eye. Another had a scar shaped like a geography lesson across his cheek.

“Name,” the woman said.

“Hungry,” Kade said. “But my friends call me Kade when they’re feeling generous.”

“Friend count must be low.”

“Tragically.”

She nodded to Scar. He moved in with zip ties. Kade didn’t fight. Not yet. The pipe clattered to the pavement, ringing like a cheap bell.

“You from the east blocks?” the woman asked as Scar wrenched Kade’s wrists behind him.

“I was from a lot of places before the drones redecorated.”

“Answers,” Scar growled. “Not poetry.”

“He can do both,” Kade said. “I’m versatile.”

She studied him—a measured, evaluating look. Discipline, yes, but something else under it. Not the dead-eyed math of a raider weighing resale value. The moral kind of math. Dangerous in a different way.

“What faction?” she asked.

“I’m unaffiliated.” He smiled. “I’m a terrible joiner. Clubs find me exhausting.”

“Unaffiliated means thief.” Mask-Dome chimed in. “Or spy.”

“Or scav just trying not to starve,” Kade said. “Wild concept.”

“Commander?” Scar asked the woman, eyes on the crossbow like it might grant wishes. “Take him to camp?”

She hesitated for half a breath—only someone watching her closely would notice. Kade was very good at watching.

“Search him,” she said.

They did: pockets tugged inside out, jacket stripped, boots checked, the whole ritual humiliation. They found a coil of wire, a cracked lighter, half a ration bar wrapped in waxed paper.

Scar held it up. “Theft,” he said, like a judge pronouncing sentence.

Kade looked at the bar and tried not to salivate. “Careful,” he said. “It bites.”

Mask-Dome unwrapped it, sniffed. “Stale.”

“Vintage,” Kade said. “Aged to perfection.”

The woman flicked her eyes from the ration to his hands. She noticed the stumps. People always did. He wiggled the shortened fingers in a little wave.

“Factory accident?” she asked.

“Post-factory accident,” he said lightly. “Long story. Fewer fingers.”

She lowered the crossbow a fraction. “Bring him,” she said.

They marched him through the ruin-grid: streets like cracked skin, buildings with their faces torn off. Ash fell in soft shivers. Somewhere distant, a drone coughed smoke and spiraled into a rooftop with all the grace of a drunken moth.

Kade counted steps, turns, sightlines out of habit. He’d been a prisoner before. He’d also stopped being a prisoner before. Hope was a bad plan; maps were better.

They reached a half-collapsed parking garage dressed up as a fortress. Rebar teeth, rag walls, a watch post made from a flipped truck bed. Inside: two dozen souls moving with tense economy. Not raiders. Too organized. Not army. Too patched.

“Down,” Scar said, shoving him to cracked concrete. Someone looped a rope from an overhead beam; Scar tied him quick and neat, looping twice around the wrists, once around the throat—not choking, but promising.

The woman took off her gloves and rubbed at the dust on her jaw. “We’ll question him after sweep,” she said to Scar. “Keep him quiet.”

Scar grinned. “With pleasure.”

She shot him a look that said not that kind of quiet, then turned to go. Kade watched her move—steady, deliberate. Commander, then. The kind who didn’t waste bullets or breath.

“Commander!” a young sentry called from the ramp. “Drone to the south, low. Might be live.”

“Positions,” she said. The camp went taut, people sliding into cover like they’d rehearsed it a hundred times. Scar left Kade tied to the beam, tossed a rag over his head as an afterthought.

Kade listened to the soft scrape of boots, the drawn bowstrings, the hush that falls when everyone waits for something that might kill them. The drone’s buzz fattened, grew brighter, then faded again, drifting off like a thought no one wanted to finish. The camp exhaled.

The rag yanked away. Scar’s grin returned. “Game time.”

“Is that what we’re calling it?” Kade said. “I was rooting for tea.”

Scar held up a knife. Not big. Ugly in a dependable way. “You can talk,” he said, “or I can make music.”

“Try a lullaby,” Kade said. “I haven’t slept well.”

Scar grabbed his jaw. “What faction. What routes. Who else is scavving our blocks.”

Kade gave him a pleasant smile. “If I had a faction, do you think I’d be eating antique candy bars? Actually—don’t answer. You don’t look like a man who thinks.”

Scar’s fist hit him once, fast. Teeth rattled. Blood ironed his tongue. He laughed, short. It hurt; he laughed anyway. People got confused when you didn’t play your part.

“Enough,” the woman said, reappearing like a change in weather. Scar stepped back, not happy about it.

“We’ll do this clean,” she told Kade. “You talk, you walk.”

“Define walk,” Kade said. “As in ‘out of here intact,’ or ‘a scenic stroll and a shallow grave’?”

“As in you leave,” she said flatly. “We have larger problems than one scav.”

“Your bedside manner,” Kade said, “is very attractive and slightly terrifying.”

Scar bristled. “Commander—”

“Later,” she said. The word closed Scar’s mouth like a latch.

She crouched to Kade’s level. Up close, he saw the tiny burn marks on her sleeves—sparks from welded drone plates. He smelled smoke, oil, and something almost floral under it. Soap? Luxury.

“What do you call yourself?” she asked.

“Kade,” he said. “You?”

Her eyes flicked—not your business—but then, unexpectedly: “Elara.”

He rolled the name on his tongue. “Elara. That’s a star name.”

“It’s a moon,” she said, reflexive. “Around Jupiter.”

“Moon, star—same neighborhood.”

“Not really.”

A corner of his mouth lifted. Under other skies, this would have been flirting. Under these, it was still flirting, just badly timed.

“You were alone?” she asked.

“Mostly,” he said.

“Mostly isn’t an answer.”

“It’s the best I’ve got.” He raised his bound wrists an inch. “My calendar’s open if you want to pencil in more questions.”

She glanced at his hands again. “What happened.”

He shrugged. “Picked a fight with a conveyor belt and lost. Long time ago.”

“You’re clever,” she said. “It gets people killed.”

“Everything gets people killed,” he said. “Clever at least dies interesting.”

Something like sympathy flickered across her face and vanished. “There’s a supply run due from the river markets,” she said, almost to herself. “If you were shadowing it—”

“I wasn’t. But if you want to trade, I know routes west that still have water if you dig for it.”

Scar snorted. “Lies.”

“Probably,” Kade said. “But it costs you nothing to hear them.”

“Everything costs,” Scar said. “Starting with your teeth.”

Elara stood. The camp’s attention drifted back toward them, little sideways looks like birds checking the sky. This was the moment—the bending point where a commander decides what kind of leader they are.

“Not here,” she said. “Put him in the east pen. I’ll decide at dusk.”

Scar’s smile came back. “Execution at sunset. Classic.”

Elara’s gaze cut to him. “I said I’ll decide.”

Scar’s smile practiced retreat. He sliced the rope from the beam and yanked Kade to his feet. “Walk, pretty boy.”

Kade stumbled as blood rushed back to his fingers, little shocks firing through nerves that never got the memo about missing pieces. The east pen turned out to be a rectangle of fencing half-swallowed by ivy, a dog kennel from when people had pets and not problems. Scar shoved him inside, tied him to the fence post. Two kids watched from behind a pallet wall with eyes too old for their faces.

“Hey,” Kade said to them, cheerful like a street clown with nothing left to lose. “If you see my dignity, tell it I want it back.”

They didn’t smile. People rarely did until after the danger passed. Sometimes not even then.

He tested the knot. Scar knew his craft. Kade cataloged his inventory: one mouth, one brain, one willingness to do something stupid if it bought time. He leaned his head back against the post and closed his eyes, listening to the camp live its careful life: the scrape of knives on metal, a kettle sighing, the soft cadence of voices that had learned to keep secrets.

Toward evening, Elara returned with a tin cup. “Water,” she said.

He eyed it. “No hidden truth serum?”

“Just water.” She held it while he drank, steady hand, no flinch when his lips brushed the rim.

“Kind of you,” he said. “I’ll try not to disappoint your investment.”

“Don’t flatter yourself,” she said, but without heat.

He studied her. Close up, the fatigue lived at the edges of her mouth. Commanders wore exhaustion like jewelry. “You could have shot me,” he said. “Simpler.”

“I’m tired of simple.” She hesitated. “And I’m tired of killing.”

“That makes two of us,” he said before he could stop himself.

She looked almost surprised—like she expected anything from him but honesty. “You don’t talk like a raider.”

“I don’t steal from people who don’t have anything left,” he said. “I just relieve inanimate objects of their burdens. Vending machines, mostly. We have a complicated relationship.”

A ghost of a smile touched her mouth. “Scar thinks we should hang you.”

“Scar and I have artistic differences.”

“Scar thinks everyone is lying.”

“Scar may be right,” Kade said. “But lies are just stories that don’t know how to end.”

Her eyes searched his, as if the answer were written there in a language she used to speak. Finally, she set the cup down.

“Dusk,” she said softly. “Stay quiet.”

“Was planning to sing,” he said. “You’ve crushed me.”

She left. The sky deepened to the color of bruised plums. Campfires pricked the gloom. Somewhere, someone hummed a lullaby off-key.

Two men came at last—Scar and Mask-Dome—with a length of chain and a self-satisfied air. Scar unlocked the pen, motioned with the knife. “Time to decide,” he said.

Elara waited by the watch post, face lit by a wavering lantern. A knot of their people stood behind her: the tired, the angry, the ones who knew what a public decision could do to a camp. Power is theater. Everyone wanted front-row seats.

Scar shoved Kade to his knees. “Say your prayers.”

“I used mine up,” Kade said. “But if you have extra—”

“Enough,” Elara said, voice carrying, quiet but iron. She looked at Kade, then at her people. “He claims unaffiliated. He knows routes. He hasn’t harmed us.”

“Yet,” Scar said.

“Yet,” she agreed. “I won’t waste a life we don’t have to waste.”

Murmurs. Mask-Dome shook his head. Someone spat. The kids from earlier watched, motionless, as if memorizing how adults choose.

Scar stepped closer to Kade, eyes bright with the sick little joy of men who think fate’s on their side. “Mercy gets you killed,” he said to Elara. “You taught us that.”

“I taught you to think,” she said. “Try it.”

He laughed, ugly. “If you won’t do it, I will.”

He lifted the knife.

Elara’s crossbow thunked before the camp could blink. The bolt buried itself in the dirt, a hair from Scar’s boot.

“Stand down,” she said.

Silence hit like cold water. Scar stared at the bolt, then at her. There was a different kind of math in his eyes now, the kind that added up pride and subtracted sense.

Elara kept the bow leveled. “We keep him tonight. At first light, he works for his water or he walks west with nothing. My decision.”

Scar’s jaw worked. He stepped back an inch, then another, filing away the insult for later.

The crowd dispersed in dissatisfied fragments. Mask-Dome muttered something about waste. The kids finally blinked.

Elara lowered the crossbow and looked at Kade like she was trying to forgive him for existing.

“You bought a very expensive night,” Kade said. “I hope it was worth it.”

“Make it worth it,” she said. “Or don’t be here at dawn.”

She turned and left him crouched in the pen, wrists burning, throat raw, heart doing unhelpful things.

Kade stared at the lantern’s flicker. The knot in his chest he called common sense tugged hard: Run if you get a chance. The quieter knot, older and more dangerous, whispered back: She didn’t kill you.

He tested the fence again—habit, not hope. Somewhere beyond the garage, a drone buzzed, lonely and lost, circling a sky that no longer wanted it.

“Same,” he murmured to the dark. “Absolutely same.”

And for the first time in a long time, he wondered what might happen if he didn’t run.

Prisoner’s Choice
Chapter Two — Ambush in the Ruins

Morning in the ruins had no real color. Just shades of ash rearranged into different disappointments.

Kade woke with the taste of rust in his mouth and the sound of the camp stirring. Pots clanged, boots scraped concrete, voices whispered like they were afraid the sky might overhear. He flexed his wrists against the ropes—no give. Scar tied knots the way some men prayed: with obsession.

Elara appeared, lean frame outlined against the fractured light filtering through the garage’s gaps. She held a bowl of something steaming. The smell was vague—grain if you were generous, boiled dust if you weren’t.

“Breakfast,” she said.

Kade sniffed theatrically. “My compliments to the chef. Tell them the ash pairs beautifully with the… ash.”

“Eat or don’t,” she said, crouching to spoon it toward him.

He leaned forward, caught the edge with his mouth. Warmth spread through him, thin but real. He tried not to moan like a man meeting soup for the first time. “If this is poison,” he said, “I’d like the recipe.”

Her lips twitched before flattening. “You’ll work for more tonight.”

Scar’s voice slashed across the pen. “He’ll work in the dirt. Then he’ll bleed in it.”

Kade turned his head. “Good morning to you too, Scar. You dream of me again?”

Scar’s jaw clenched. Elara shot him a warning look.

“Patrol sweep?” she asked.

“South block’s quiet,” Scar said. “East alleys are empty. West…” He flicked a glance at Kade. “…could be trouble.”

“Then we’ll check it,” she said.

Scar barked a laugh. “With him? Dragging him along is dead weight.”

“He knows the ground,” she said simply.

Everyone looked at her. Scar’s expression soured like milk in the sun.

“Commander—”

“Decision’s made.”

And that was that.

They cut him loose, bound his wrists again, and marched him with the patrol. The ruined streets unfolded: husks of shops with mannequins half-buried in rubble, cars gutted and rusted into cages. Sunlight slipped through gaps like a thief.

Kade counted five in the squad: Scar, Mask-Dome, two younger fighters trying hard to look older, and Elara leading with her crossbow. He walked between them, rope tugging at his wrists, his sarcasm his only weapon.

“So,” he said lightly, “is this the scenic tour, or do I have to pay extra for landmarks?”

“Shut up,” Scar growled.

“I’d love to, but it’s a medical condition. Chronic commentary.”

One of the younger ones snorted before catching Scar’s glare. Kade winked at him.

They reached a shattered intersection, the skeleton of a drone factory looming like a broken jaw. The air smelled of burnt plastic and rust. Elara raised her hand: stop.

The world went quiet. Too quiet.

Kade felt it first—the prickling on the back of his neck, the hush that came before a bad thing showed its teeth.

And then the rubble moved.

Figures poured out of the collapsed factory: raiders, lean and fast, eyes bright with hunger. They carried machetes, pipes, jagged metal that barely qualified as weapons but promised pain anyway.

“Ambush,” Scar hissed, stating the obvious.

“Positions!” Elara snapped, already dropping to one knee, crossbow aimed.

The raiders screamed, a sound that was more animal than human, and rushed.

The fight exploded.

Scar barreled forward with a roar, knife flashing. Mask-Dome swung a pipe, denting a raider’s skull. The kids tried to keep formation, terrified and furious in equal measure.

Elara’s crossbow sang, bolt slamming into a raider’s chest. She moved with precision, calm in the storm.

Kade? Kade had rope on his wrists and no weapon but his mouth. He ducked as a raider swung a cleaver, barely missing his head. He rammed his shoulder into the man, sending them both sprawling. The rope burned, but adrenaline burned hotter.

“Little help?” he shouted.

Elara shot the raider clean through the thigh. The man screamed, dropped. She yanked Kade upright with surprising strength.

“Stay close,” she barked.

He grinned through blood and sweat. “You had me at ‘stay.’”

The battle was short and ugly. Raiders scattered after half a dozen fell, the rest limping or crawling back into the factory’s shadow. Scar wanted to chase; Elara held him back.

“Waste of bolts,” she said.

Scar spat on the ground, eyes dark. “He slowed us down.”

“He distracted them,” Elara countered. “Without it, we’d have lost one of the kids.”

Kade raised his bound hands. “You’re welcome. Payment accepted in food, blankets, or small statues carved in my likeness.”

Scar lunged for him. Elara stepped between, steady as stone.

“Enough,” she said. “He stays. For now.”

Scar glared at her, jaw clenched so hard Kade wondered if it might crack.

The squad regrouped, shaken but alive. One of the kids had a shallow cut across his arm. Elara bound it quick, efficient, hands steady.

Kade watched her work, something stirring uncomfortably in his chest. She wasn’t just commanding—she was protecting. That kind of compassion got people killed. He knew because it had nearly killed him before.

When she finished, she looked at him. “You fight with your head. Not bad.”

“I’d fight with my hands too,” he said, “but Scar’s into rope tricks.”

Her mouth almost curved. Almost. “We’ll cut you loose tonight. If you don’t run.”

Kade tilted his head. “Run where? The world’s already ended. Might as well end here.”

They marched back to camp, smoke rising behind them from the bodies left to rot. The squad was quieter now, the air heavy with things unsaid. Scar seethed, Elara brooded, Kade limped but smiled anyway.

Inside, the camp’s eyes followed them—counting who returned, who didn’t. Survival was theater, and every act was judged.

Scar threw his knife into a post, hard. “He’ll betray us.”

“Or he’ll save us,” Elara said.

And just like that, the lines were drawn.

Kade sat against the pen again, wrists raw, but his grin intact. He’d survived another day, made himself useful, and planted a seed in Elara’s mind she couldn’t quite uproot.

And maybe—just maybe—he wanted her to water it.

Chapter Three — The Prisoner

The pen smelled like rust and smoke. Kade leaned against the fence post, wrists raw, head tilted like he was napping. In truth, he was listening. Camps spoke in sounds: the scrape of a pot meant food was scarce; the hiss of oil on metal meant someone was repairing; the silence of children meant fear.

He’d heard too many silences.

Scar loomed outside the pen, arms folded, scarred cheek pulled tight like old leather. He stared the way men stared at problems they couldn’t solve without a knife.

“You don’t belong here,” Scar said.

“I’ve heard that before,” Kade said. “Usually right before someone makes me leave. Forcefully. With fewer teeth.”

“Don’t worry,” Scar said. “We’ll keep the pattern.”

Before Kade could reply, Elara appeared, her shadow stretching long in the ashlight. She carried a ledger—actual paper, bound in frayed leather, pages crammed with cramped handwriting. The sight of it made Kade blink. Paper meant history. Records. Discipline.

She crouched by the fence. “Routes,” she said.

“Straight to business,” Kade said. “Do you ever flirt, or is that a war crime now?”

“Routes,” she repeated.

He lifted his hands. “Hard to draw maps while tied up. Unless you’re fond of interpretive dance.”

Her eyes narrowed, but not in anger. In calculation. “Talk, then.”

Kade sighed, leaned back. “West roads are mined. East has raider nests. South collapses into nothing past the viaduct. If you want food, you head north. River markets. Risky, but better than starving.”

Scar barked a laugh. “River markets? Dead or worse.”

“Not dead,” Kade said. “Not yet. I got water there last month. Paid with a lighter and a smile.”

Elara studied him. “And why tell us?”

“Because if you’re going to kill me, I’d rather you do it on a full stomach.” He flashed a grin. “Call it generosity.”

Scar slammed his fist into the fence. The post rattled. “He lies.”

Elara’s gaze didn’t move. “Maybe. But lies have edges. You can cut with them if you know where to hold.”

Scar stepped closer. “You’re protecting him.”

Her jaw tightened. “I’m using him.”

Kade’s grin widened. “I feel so valued.”

Scar spat, then stormed off, muttering about wasted bolts.

Elara remained. She rested the ledger on her knee, fingers brushing its spine. For a moment, she seemed less like a commander, more like someone carrying too much weight.

“You should have run when you escaped,” she said softly.

“I did,” Kade said. “Then I came back.”

“Why?”

He shrugged. “I owed you. You didn’t kill me. That’s rare these days. Almost charming.”

Her lips twitched—the ghost of a smile, gone as fast as it came.

“You’re reckless,” she said.

“Reckless people live longer than you’d think,” Kade said. “We’re too stupid to die properly.”

She stood, closing the ledger. “We’ll see.”

As she walked away, Kade leaned his head back against the post and exhaled.

The smart thing would be to plan an escape. Scar wanted him dead, the camp didn’t trust him, and Elara’s protection was fragile at best.

But he wasn’t planning an escape.

He was planning how not to waste the one person who looked at him like he was still human.

Prisoner’s Choice
Chapter Four — The Choice

Dusk painted the ruins the color of dried blood. Fires sparked to life inside the garage fortress, thin curls of smoke escaping into the bruised sky. The camp gathered in a rough circle around the pen, their faces lit by firelight, their eyes harder than steel.

Scar stood at the front, knife in hand, his grin wolfish. “It’s simple,” he said. “He’s a scav. A thief. Probably a spy. The longer he breathes, the hungrier we get. You know the math.”

Murmurs rose—agreement, anger, fear. Hunger made truth a weapon sharper than any blade.

Elara stepped forward, crossbow slung across her back, ledger tucked under one arm. She looked at her people, not at Kade. “And what does killing him solve? One less mouth? One less body in the world? It won’t feed us. It won’t repair the wells. It won’t stop drones from burning the sky.”

Scar sneered. “But it’ll keep us safe.”

“Safe?” Kade said from the pen, voice dry. “If you think killing me makes you safe, I have bad news about the world outside your little fence.”

“Shut him up,” Scar snapped.

Elara lifted a hand. “Let him speak.”

The camp shifted uneasily. No one liked a prisoner who sounded too human.

Kade leaned forward, ropes biting into his wrists. “You want to know what I am? I’m hungry. I’m broken. Just like you. If I was a spy, don’t you think I’d have better shoes?” He kicked one boot out through the fence—sole peeling, laces knotted with wire. “This is the uniform of a man with no one left to fight for but himself. Congratulations—you’ve got yourselves the least impressive prisoner in history.”

A ripple of reluctant laughter moved through the crowd. Scar’s face darkened.

“Mercy gets people killed,” he barked. “We survive because we’re ruthless.”

“We survive because we’re smart,” Elara said, voice cutting clean. “Because we choose differently than the world that burned.”

Scar stepped closer, knife raised. “Then choose, Commander. Him or us.”

The camp went still. Even the fires seemed to hush, waiting.

Elara met Scar’s eyes. Her hand moved slow and deliberate as she slid the crossbow from her back. She raised it—not at Kade, but at Scar.

Gasps rippled through the circle.

“You want blood?” she said. “Take mine first.”

Scar froze. His knife wavered.

“This isn’t mercy,” Elara continued. “It’s strategy. He has routes. He has knowledge. And until I say otherwise, he has value. That’s my choice.”

Scar’s jaw worked, teeth grinding. He lowered the knife, but the fury in his eyes promised this wasn’t over.

The camp broke apart in mutters and sidelong looks, some approving, most unsettled. Fear of drones was one thing. Fear of division was worse.

Elara lowered the crossbow, her shoulders heavy with the weight of command. She turned to Kade.

“You live,” she said flatly.

Kade tilted his head. “Romantic. Usually people buy me flowers before declarations like that.”

A ghost of a smile flickered across her face before vanishing. She turned and walked away, leaving him tied to the post, firelight flickering across his bruises.

For the first time in years, Kade didn’t feel like prey. He felt like a gamble someone had just staked everything on.

And he couldn’t decide if that was better or worse.

Chapter Four — The Choice

And in the ashes, war waited.
